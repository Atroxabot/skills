# Cold Outreach Writer

Write short, specific, honest messages to people who don't know the sender yet. The message should give the reader a real reason to reply and an easy way to say no.

The research behind this skill was done in October 2026. [references/rules.md](references/rules.md) has the benchmarks and the deliverability, legal and DM details, with dated sources.

## What actually decides replies

- **Relevance beats cleverness.** The top reasons decision makers ignore cold email: too sales-focused (65%), irrelevant (61%), generic (48%). Two specific, checkable "why you, why now" details lifted replies from 3.6% to 5.6% (Hunter, 2025 data).
- **Short, plain, one ask.** First emails do best at about 50–100 words (under 80 for executives), in plain text, with no images, attachments or tracking. Emails without open or link tracking got 7.4% replies, against 4.4% with it.
- **Ask for interest, not a meeting.** "Interest" asks beat meeting requests at the cold stage (Gong, 304K emails). Sell the conversation, not the calendar slot.
- **Follow-ups matter.** About 40–60% of replies come after the first email, and three messages roughly doubled replies against one. Each follow-up needs a new angle.
- **Complaints decide delivery.** Gmail and Yahoo require spam complaints under 0.3% (Google aims for 0.1%), which is 1–3 complaints per 1,000 emails, and since November 2025 Gmail rejects mail that breaks its rules. So relevance and an easy opt-out aren't just good manners: they keep the sender's email working.
- **AI-written outreach gets noticed.** 69% of US decision makers say AI-written emails bother them, and hand-edited emails beat fully automated ones.

Realistic benchmarks: about 3–5% replies is average, 5–10% good, 10% and up top decile. Always ask what the number is divided by, because studies count per email, per contact or per sequence.

## Workflow

### 1. Get what you need, and refuse to invent it

Collect:
- **Who the recipient is:** role, company, and why they in particular.
- **The trigger or observation:** something specific and checkable from the last 30–90 days, such as a job post, launch, funding round, a stated priority, or a tool they adopted.
- **The problem the sender solves,** in the recipient's terms.
- **One proof point:** a similar customer and a concrete result, a number, or how it works.
- **The ask:** what a "yes" leads to.
- **The sender:** name, role, company and voice. If there's a voice card or style guide (a `voice-profile.md` in the working directory, or one the user pastes), read it. It overrides generic style defaults, and facts it lists about the sender are true material.
- **Where the recipients are:** the country decides which footer lines the law requires (step 3).

**Never invent** facts about the recipient (posts, quotes, funding, hires), mutual connections, referrals, customer names or results. In outreach a made-up detail is caught at once and kills trust, and a fake referral can be a legal problem too. If a detail is missing, write `[placeholder: what to check]` and say where to find it. Keep it to one or two placeholders; if more are missing, ask.

If the user wants to blast a big generic list, say plainly what the data shows: sequences to 21–50 recipients got 6.2% replies against 2.4% for 500 or more, and emailing one or two people per company beat three or more. Then help them narrow the list.

### 2. Pick the framework

Skeletons and examples are in [references/frameworks.md](references/frameworks.md):

| Situation | Framework |
|---|---|
| Default first touch | Observation → Problem → Proof → Ask |
| Senior reader, little time | Three sentences |
| A clear recent event | Trigger-event email |
| Not sure who owns it | Right-person ask |
| A sharp, costly problem | PAS, with one factual line of "agitate" |
| LinkedIn connection | Connection note (200 characters or fewer) |
| After they accept, or on X | DM with no pitch first |

### 3. Write the email

**Subject line**
- 2–5 words, specific to their situation, in sentence case or lowercase so it looks like a colleague's email.
- Name the problem, the trigger or a shared detail, not your product.
- No "Quick question," no clickbait, no fake "Re:" or "Fwd:", no urgency.
- It must match the body. A misleading subject line is a legal problem as well as a trust one.

**First line**
- About them, not you. It's also the inbox preview.
- Never "I hope this finds you well," "My name is…," "I came across your profile," or "I wanted to reach out."

**Body**
- 50–100 words. One problem, one proof point, one ask.
- Plain words, short sentences, about a 3rd–5th grade reading level. It should read like a person typed it on their phone.
- No buzzwords ("revolutionary," "best-in-class," "synergy," "streamline," "leverage") and no vague ROI claims; both measurably cut replies.
- No images, attachments, HTML banners, calendar links or link shorteners, since all of them hurt delivery or trust. Ideally no link at all: offer the resource instead ("Want me to send it?").

**The ask**
- One low-friction question with an easy yes or no: "Worth sending over how [peer] handled it?" or "Is this on your radar this quarter?"
- Not a 30-minute meeting, and no time slots yet.

**Sign-off and footer**
- The sender's real name and company, and a plain opt-out line ("If this isn't relevant, just reply 'no' and I won't follow up.").
- A postal address for US and Canadian recipients.
- For EU and UK recipients: where the address came from, and a link to a privacy notice.

**Every email in a sequence carries the footer,** follow-ups included, because the rules apply to each message. CAN-SPAM also requires commercial email to be identifiable as an advertisement; the FTC allows flexibility in how, so point US senders to the FTC guide rather than inventing a label. The legal table is in [references/rules.md](references/rules.md). Don't call a message "compliant." Say what's included, and suggest checking local rules (Germany and Canada are stricter).

### 4. Write the follow-ups (when asked for a sequence)

- **3–5 emails over about 2–3 weeks.** The first follow-up comes 2–4 days later, later ones 4–7 days apart. Stop at the first reply of any kind.
- **Every follow-up adds something:** a different angle, a proof point, a useful resource, or a short question. Never "just checking in," "bumping this," or "circling back"; empty bumps reportedly cut meetings booked.
- **Follow-ups are shorter than the opener,** often 1–3 sentences. Early ones can reply in the same thread (a genuine "Re:").
- **The last touch is polite and gives an easy out.** No guilt trips ("Did you fall off a cliff?", "Should I close your file?") and no fake deadlines.
- **More than five touches raises complaints sharply.** Advise against it.

### 5. LinkedIn and X messages

- **Send by hand.** LinkedIn's User Agreement bans bots and automation for messages and invitations, and accounts that use them get restricted. Never suggest automation tools or scraping.
- **Connection notes: 200 characters or fewer.** Give one reason to connect that's about them (shared context, their specific work, a mutual person). No pitch, no link.
- **The first DM after they accept doesn't pitch.** Thank them, mention why you connected, and ask a light question. 2–4 short lines that read like chat.
- **InMail** reads like a short cold email: a subject, 3–5 sentences, one ask, under about 400 characters.
- **X:** engage in public first (a substantive reply to their post), then send a 1–3-sentence DM that refers to that exchange. No link and no pitch in the first message.

### 6. Edit like a person wrote it

Read [references/ai-writing-signs.md](references/ai-writing-signs.md) and fix causes, not words. Outreach has its own tells:
- "I noticed that [company] is [generic statement]"
- three stacked compliments
- "It's not X, it's Y"
- rule-of-three benefit lists
- "Would love to explore synergies"
- a polished, formal tone for a two-line note

Read it aloud. If you wouldn't say it to them at a conference, rewrite it. If the humanizer skill is installed, it can do a deeper pass.

If you can run code, check drafts with the outreach linter:

```
python3 scripts/lint_email.py email.txt
python3 scripts/lint_email.py sequence.txt --sequence      # emails separated by a line holding only ---
python3 scripts/lint_email.py note.txt --linkedin-note     # connection notes
```

It checks the word count and subject length; links, attachments and calendar links; worn-out openers and follow-ups; fake "Re:" and urgency; the opt-out line; and AI-writing signs. A warning means look again, not rewrite automatically.

## Output

Unless the user asks otherwise, return:

1. **The email(s),** each with the subject line, body and footer, in a code block so it pastes cleanly. Give the word count of the message itself, not counting the sign-off and footer.
2. **Two alternative subject lines,** and one alternative first line if the opener is the riskiest part.
3. **For sequences:** each step labelled with its send day (Day 1, Day 4, Day 9…) and the new angle it adds.
4. **Notes,** three bullets at most:
   - `[placeholders]` to verify
   - any legal lines the user must add for their region
   - one targeting tip if the list looks too broad

**When reviewing the user's own outreach:** diagnose the subject, first line, relevance, length, ask, follow-ups and AI tells, quoting their lines. Then rewrite it, keeping every true detail.

**When replies are low across the board,** ask about the list and delivery before blaming the copy: fewer than 1% replies per email usually means a targeting or deliverability problem. If the mail may be going to spam, the email-dns-auth skill, if installed, checks the sending domain's SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

## Final check

- [ ] The first line is about them, with one specific, checkable "why you, why now"
- [ ] Every fact came from the user or is a marked `[placeholder]`
- [ ] 50–100 words (first email), plain text, one ask, no calendar link, at most one link
- [ ] The subject line is 2–5 words, specific and honest
- [ ] Footer on every email: real name and company, a plain opt-out, and an address where required
- [ ] Each follow-up adds something new; the last one is polite; five touches or fewer
- [ ] It reads like a person, with no buzzwords or AI tells
