# LinkedIn hooks

The hook is everything before the "see more" cut: about 140 characters on mobile and about 210 on desktop, which in practice means the first one or two lines. Its only job is to make the right reader click. The rest of the post's job is to make them glad they did.

## What the data says

- **Line one should be short.** In AuthoredUp's dataset of 771K posts, first lines under 40 characters had a median engagement of 2.61%, against 2.08% for first lines over 200 characters.
- **Questions are the weakest hook type.** In AuthoredUp's 309K posts (Dec 2025–May 2026), story hooks had a median engagement of 2.60%, contrarian hooks 2.31% and question hooks 2.16%.
- **Fewer than 10 words.** One smaller study (about 1K posts) found these hooks got about 40% more "see more" clicks.
- **Line one isn't the whole hook.** Justin Welsh breaks the opening into three jobs: line one stops the scroll, line two adds context or credibility (it took time, or cost something), and line three, right before the cut, makes the click irresistible.

Dated sources for these figures are in [platform.md](platform.md).

## Principles

1. **Clear beats clever.** From the hook alone, a reader should know what the post is about and who it's for. Puns and vague teases lose to plain specifics.
2. **Specifics create belief.** Numbers, names, timeframes and money signal a real story. Compare "I made a costly hiring mistake" with "My first hire cost us $40k and 6 months."
3. **Tension creates the click.** Open a loop: a surprising result with the cause held back, a stake, a contradiction, or the moment just before something happened.
4. **Claims beat questions.** Rewrite question hooks as statements.
5. **Never bait.** Don't promise what the post doesn't deliver, and don't shock for its own sake. A click without a payoff costs reading time and trust.

## Formulas

Each formula has a template and an example. The examples are illustrative and invented for this file; never present their details as the user's own.

**1. Specific event + stakes**
`[Something that happened], [when it happened / what was at stake].`
> I got fired the week we hit $1M ARR.

**2. Result with the cause held back**
`[Small thing] [big measurable result].`
> One line in my cold email tripled my reply rate.

**3. Contrarian imperative**
`Stop [common practice].` or `[Popular advice] is wrong.`
> Stop asking for feedback on your resume.

**4. Dated anecdote**
`[Time ago], [specific person] [did specific thing].`
> Two years ago, a customer emailed to ask why our tool was so cheap.

**5. The ladder**
Matt Barker's method: start with a bare claim and add one element at a time (a timeframe, then a number, then an outcome, then a twist). Stop when it's specific enough to surprise.
> I write posts. → I write 7 posts a week. → I write 7 posts in 20 minutes. → I write a week of posts in 20 minutes, and they outperform the ones I sweat over.

**6. How to [outcome] without [obstacle]**
> How to get a promotion without asking for one.

Variant: `How to [outcome] in under [time].`

**7. Villain → momentum → stance**
Also from Justin Welsh: line one names something the reader dislikes, line two shows it's changing, line three gives the author's stance and a reason to click.
> Status meetings are dying.
> Three of our five teams dropped them this year.
> Nobody misses them. Here's what replaced them.

**8. Data observation**
`We [looked at N things]. [Surprising finding].`
> We reviewed 400 sales calls. Top reps talked less than 45% of the time.

**9. Confession or mistake with a cost**
`My [mistake] cost [specific cost]. [What I'd do now].`
> My first hire cost us $40k and 6 months. Here's the interview question I'd add.

**10. Compressed before/after**
`[Then]: [state]. [Now]: [state].`
> 2023: 0 clients, 9 hours a day of cold DMs.
> 2026: a waitlist and zero outbound.

**11. Pattern observation**
`Every [group] I've [met / worked with] [does the same specific thing].`
> Every great manager I've had did the same thing in 1:1s.

**12. Strong declarative**
`[Category] [blunt claim].`
> Most job descriptions are written for the wrong person.

**13. Moment in time**
`[Specific moment], [what was about to happen].`
> 11:47pm, the night before launch, our payment provider froze our account.

**14. Number + promise**
`[N] [things] that [specific outcome] (from [credible source of experience]).`
> 7 questions that got me better answers in every customer interview I ran last year.

## Second lines

Line two should raise the stakes or add credibility, not repeat line one.

- Add proof of effort: "It took 3 years and 2 failed products to figure out why."
- Add stakes: "We had 9 days of cash left."
- Add a twist: "The fix was to make fewer calls."
- Promise a payoff: "Here's the exact script."

## Before and after

| Weak | Why | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| I'm excited to share some lessons I've learned about leadership over the years. | Throat-clearing, vague, no tension | I managed 40 people before anyone taught me how to run a 1:1. |
| Have you ever wondered what makes a great product manager? | Question hook, generic | The best PM I ever worked with never wrote a PRD. |
| In today's fast-paced world, networking is more important than ever. | Cliché, AI tell, no claim | I got my last three jobs from people I helped for free. |
| Hiring is hard. | True but empty | We interviewed 212 people to make 3 hires. Here's the one question that mattered. |
| It's not about working harder, it's about working smarter. | AI reframe tic, platitude | I cut my work week to 32 hours. Revenue went up 18%. |

## Checklist

- [ ] It's complete within ~140 characters (the mobile preview)
- [ ] Line one is short, ideally under ~40 characters
- [ ] It has at least one specific: a number, name, time or stake
- [ ] It makes a claim or tells a moment; it doesn't ask a generic question
- [ ] The post delivers what the hook promises
- [ ] It's not a cliché, an AI tic or engagement bait
